Well, tomorrow (as this is being written) or yesterday (as you are reading it) is my 60th birthday, and something like my 37th year in this wild industry. It&#8217;s funny to think that when I was a punk programmer in my late 20&#8217;s, I had quite the sense of urgency because &#8220;everyone knew&#8221; that programmers were just DONE in their mid-30&#8217;s, and I didn&#8217;t have much time left. The irony is that I&#8217;ve both &#8220;written&#8221; (let&#8217;s say caused to be created) more code in the last 3 months than in the entire rest of my career pre-AI, and also took a codebase from that very beginning of time, Spectre, written in Pascal for the Mac Classic, and tossed it at Claude to be ported, perfectly in one shot, to JavaScript and html 1 . It&#8217;s mind blowing to me that something written for such a completely different architecture (software and hardware) and performance profile could be ported that well. That example, Spectre, led me to build some other games, and that led me to the idea I want to share this week: how (and what) we should teach in the modern era. It&#8217;s hard to know - it doesn&#8217;t seem like it makes a ton of sense to teach the craft of writing code when code can be written so easily for you. And, in fact, I didn&#8217;t learn to write code much in school - I took a single data structures class, but that was it. Everything else I learned about programming was either self-taught, first-principles I applied from my math degree, or learned on the job. So I might be an argument for not teaching coding any more. But that&#8217;s &#8230; not quite right either. It&#8217;s valuable to understand how code works, and to think clearly about what you want. In fact, this is even more broadly true of AI, at least right now: you have to be at least enough of an expert to ask good questions and spot bad answers, to get useful results. That&#8217;s not always true, of course. For some small things, you can just ask. For the Spectre port, I might as well have grunted: &#8220;me make game long ago. here old game archive. make game for me play now&#8221; and it probably would have worked. But for bigger things you have to know what you&#8217;re doing. But what, exactly, do you have to know? The best model I have found so far is that you have to be able to think like an executive. Not a manager , but an actual executive of a large organization. Why is that? Well, executives work at a scale where they can&#8217;t see all of the work being done, often don&#8217;t understand it as well as the people working for them, can&#8217;t communicate with everyone all the time (listening or directing) and often have to manage complex and contradictory incentives. And mostly, executives have to know what they want and have purpose and coherence in asking for it. All of these apply to working with larger AI systems. You are the scarce resource, so you can&#8217;t really pay attention to everything (at least if you want to scale at all). You have to learn to ask questions, set metrics, be skeptical, learn what you need to learn and how to learn it, know what you don&#8217;t know and when it&#8217;s important to educate yourself, work via lieutenants, process and metrics, and communicate effectively across a broad set of actors. We don&#8217;t really teach this right now. There are business schools that teach how to manage, but it&#8217;s hard to teach leadership and initiative. There are executive schools that take folks who have selected into senior leadership and try to help polish some aspect of them. But it seems presumptuous and weird to say &#8220;we are going to start with uneducated kids and directly teach them to think like and be executives&#8221;. It&#8217;s hard to get your head around 2 . How do you teach someone to think like a leader before they know how to do anything else? I don&#8217;t know. But I think that something like that is where education is heading. Right now, though, I see the same pattern with education that I saw in the early days of the cloud. Looking backwards through the lens of today, it&#8217;s easy to understand the idea of an application being delivered in the browser, hosted elsewhere. But in the moment, telling someone &#8220;You&#8217;re editing in a browser but not the web page, a document but actually the web page is an application and there are many documents just not right here, and also you&#8217;re working directly with someone else at the same time&#8221; is really confusing. There&#8217;s just not enough reference point at first to understand it. I think that&#8217;s where education still is. To say something like &#8220;you don&#8217;t need facts but you still need to understand how the facts work and you can direct the models to do things, but you have to supervise and be an executive before even being a worker&#8221; is about as nonsensical sounding right now. But something like it will seem very obvious in 20 years (hopefully less - it didn&#8217;t take very long for the cloud to make sense). Last week I wrote that hard things are good 3 - it&#8217;s ok, because that means there is value to be created. I think it&#8217;s fine that education is being disrupted and the answers aren&#8217;t immediately obvious - for that very reason. Figuring out how to teach this is one of those hard things. We can start with experiments like the games, see what students actually learn, and work from there. We don&#8217;t have to figure out the whole future of education before trying a different assignment. Happily, I&#8217;m still a student too. I am so grateful that I have had a career where I could learn and explore every day. I love it. Even with my experience, though, I still have to understand what matters or doesn&#8217;t, and what to pay attention to. That seems like a pretty good assignment for the kids, too. When I was in my late 20&#8217;s, I thought I had a few years left to do everything I wanted to do. At 60, I&#8217;m having trouble deciding what to try next, there are so many interesting things to learn, try and build. I know there&#8217;s a lot of fear and concern about AI. I hope others can find that same sense of optimism and exploration. I&#8217;d like school to leave more people feeling that way. Thanks for reading Sunday Letters!
