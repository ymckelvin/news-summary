Qualcomm&#8217;s laptop effort had humble beginnings. Their early Arm-based laptops used cell phone SoCs, and suffered from noticeably poor performance even against lower end Intel chips. But breaking into the PC market was going to be a marathon rather than a sprint, and Qualcomm has been persistent. Qualcomm created higher performance Snapdragon variants targeted at laptops, which became increasingly competent with each generation. Now, Qualcomm&#8217;s Snapdragon X2 Elite Extreme can deliver CPU performance that challenges the best of its x86-64 competition. To deliver that performance, Qualcomm relies on the third generation of their in-house Oryon architecture, which I&#8217;ll call Oryon 3 for short. The Snapdragon X2 Elite deploys Oryon 3 in both E-Core and P-Core variants, which Qualcomm confusingly calls &#8220;performance&#8221; and &#8220;prime&#8221; cores, respectively. P-Cores are the star of the show in the Snapdragon X2 Elite, where they provide both maximum single threaded performance while also providing the bulk of multithreaded throughput. That&#8217;s because Qualcomm has P-Cores outnumber their E-Cores by a factor of two, creating an inverted hybrid strategy compared to Intel&#8217;s. Thanks to this strategy, Qualcomm&#8217;s Oryon 3 P-Core variant shoulders most of the burden when it comes to Qualcomm&#8217;s PC market ambitions. Oryon 3 takes on this burden by deploying an architecture with a strong resemblance to the first Oryon generation. Qualcomm technically has a second generation Oryon core, but only used it in phones. Their website also suggests it&#8217;s a very minor change over the first generation, so I&#8217;ll be treating first generation Oryon as Oryon 3&#8217;s direct predecessor. Similarity to Oryon 1 is a good thing, because that gives Oryon 3 just about everything you could ask for from a 2026-era high performance design. From Oryon 1, Oryon 3 inherits an out-of-order window in excess of 600 micro-ops. Qualcomm has also carried forward a two-level cache setup that emphasizes speed over capacity. Compared to its predecessor, Oryon 3 takes things up a notch by pushing clock speeds to 5 GHz. It&#8217;s a huge 16% increase over the 4.3 GHz speed that Oryon 1 could reach, and is enough to offer a generational performance gain on its own. But Qualcomm doesn&#8217;t stop there. Oryon 3 increases core width to 9 micro-ops, up from 8 in its predecessor, and makes a number of improvements throughout the pipeline. Qualcomm brings SVE2 and SME support to the new core as well, keeping it up-to-date with the evolving aarch64 architecture and opening up new software optimization opportunities. In its role as a maximum performance architecture, Oryon 3 faces off against the likes of AMD&#8217;s Zen 5 and Intel&#8217;s Lion Cove. Arm&#8217;s Cortex X925 is another competitor, thanks to Nvidia&#8217;s laptop push with N1X. A Faster Frontend Setting aside clock speed, Oryon 3&#8217;s headline architectural improvement is a core width increase from 8 to 9 ups. At the frontend, Qualcomm supports this by adding another decode slot. The fetch and decode pipeline otherwise looks mostly unchanged. Oryon 1 already had over-provisioned instruction cache bandwidth at 64 bytes per cycle, which is more than sufficient to feed a 8-wide or 9-wide aarch64 decoder. Qualcomm has also kept a 192 KB, 6-way instruction cache with hardware coherency, as well as a 256 entry 8-way instruction TLB. These large structures should help the core use its fastest instruction delivery path more often than its peers, which have smaller instruction caches and instruction TLBs. Impressively, Qualcomm managed to keep these large structures and add an extra decode slot while keeping a 13 cycle branch mispredict penalty. Clock speed increases often come with compromises like longer pipelines or smaller structures, but here Qualcomm is able to have their cake and eat it too. Testing instruction bandwidth with a stream of NOPs validates the increased decoder width when the test loop fits within the instruction cache. Improvements continue at larger instruction footprints. Oryon 3 is able to sustain about 4 NOPs/cycle from L2, compared to 2 per cycle on Oryon 1. Qualcomm might have changed something with instruction prefetching from L2, or they might have widened datapaths between the instruction cache and L2. Whatever the case, Oryon 3 now matches the likes of AMD&#8217;s Zen 5 and Intel&#8217;s Lion Coves in terms of streaming code from L2, while boasting a much larger L2. AMD and Intel&#8217;s cores do have a L3 that provides more capacity than Qualcomm&#8217;s L2, but fetching code from that L3 will cause another drop in instruction throughput. Zen 5 curiously can't hit 8 IPC from one thread with 4B NOPs, though it can easily do so with larger 8B NOPs. Oryon 3 improves by expanding its predecessor&#8217;s ability to handle taken branches back-to-back. Oryon 1 could only do &#8220;zero-bubble&#8221; taken branches within a 8 KB instruction footprint. Oryon 3 can do so within a 2048 branch footprint, regardless of branch spacing and code footprint. Perhaps Qualcomm switched to using a fully decoupled 2048 entry BTB. Realistically, that means Oryon 3 should be able to do zero-bubble taken branches over a branch footprint 2x to 4x larger than its predecessor, considering typical branch spacing. Compared to AMD and Intel&#8217;s latest cores, Qualcomm lacks any ability to handle more than one taken branch per cycle. Arm&#8217;s newer cores are also capable of doing two taken branches per cycle. I suspect AMD, Arm, and Intel decided that handling multiple taken branches per cycle became more profitable once their cores got wide enough, so seeing Qualcomm build a very wide core without that ability is surprising. Perhaps clock speed factors into that decision, because going from 4.3 to 5 GHz is a huge jump. If so, Qualcomm&#8217;s choice would be justified because a clock speed increase of that magnitude probably offers a larger performance increase than going for two taken branches per cycle. Of course, it&#8217;s impossible to know if branch predictor paths presented a timing challenge for Oryon. For branches with dynamic taken/not-taken behavior, Oryon 3 has a 2-cycle conditional predictor. Testing with branches whose outcomes correlate with increasingly long history lengths shows an average 0.5 cycle increase in time per branch over an always- taken reference run, as long as the test stays within the conditional predictor&#8217;s capabilities. Because I randomly generate patterns so that each branch is taken roughly 50% of the time, a 0.5 cycle average penalty suggests Qualcomm only incurs the 2-cycle timing if the direction predictor disagrees with a base prediction. Direction prediction starts falling apart with more than 32K patterns in play, which is an improvement over the approximately 24K patterns that first generation Oryon could handle in this test. For comparison, Zen 5&#8217;s conditional predictor can handle roughly 48K patterns before falling apart. Unlike Oryon 3, and unlike some older Zen generations, Zen 5 does not show an increase in branch latency compared to an always-taken pattern. Indirect branches can go to multiple targets, and present another level of difficulty because the branch predictor has to select between previously seen targets, and must store more than one target per branch. Oryon 3 has a 2-cycle indirect predictor that can track 2048 indirect targets, matching its predecessor. A single branch can jump to 256 targets without incurring a significant penalty. This could mean at least eight of the bits that Oryon 3 uses to index into its indirect target array are influenced by branch history.
