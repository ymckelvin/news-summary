As I, and others around me, work more and more with larger clusters of more independent AI systems (which we will call agents here, even though I don&#8217;t love the personification), one of the things that has become apparent to me is that managing a swarm of agents is very much like being an executive. Why? When you run a large organization, it is, by definition, somewhat opaque to you: you can&#8217;t see all of what people are doing or saying (if you could, you wouldn&#8217;t need the team, you&#8217;d just do the work yourself). You have to manage and lead through a mixture of techniques: incentives, communication, hierarchy, process, critical metrics, etc. And working with other executives is particularly hard - you have to be able to translate your world into theirs in an attention-efficient way, otherwise they might as well just do your job with their team. This is a challenge for many reasons - most people aren&#8217;t comfortable with, or trained in, the kind of abstract, indirect thinking that goes into managing a somewhat opaque blob of agents (people or AI) that have their own perspectives, incentives, misunderstandings, skills (or non skills) etc. It&#8217;s a really hard job - and we don&#8217;t really train people for it either. It&#8217;s hard to know who can do it until they&#8217;ve done some leadership, so we usually train after the fact. This is starting to be a challenge - you can already see some people getting much stronger advantage from AI than average because they have good native &#8220;executive skills&#8221;. And not everyone can or will have those skills. So it&#8217;s tempting to say &#8220;well, we&#8217;ll just make the AI smarter and have it do some of that for the human&#8221;. But that doesn&#8217;t entirely work either. One of the counterintuitive things you learn as an executive is that having a smarter and more capable team often makes your job harder. Why? Shouldn&#8217;t they just be taking up more of the slack and getting things done competently? Sure, and they do, at first. But two things happen. One is that the organization will always either expand its ambitions or shrink its staff until it&#8217;s stretched to capacity - this is just a natural pattern. And it&#8217;s usually expand, not contract, so the natural state is that you are at the edge of what the organization can do. But more interestingly, and importantly, is the fact that you exist to handle exceptions that your team can&#8217;t. And if your team is smart and capable, the things they can&#8217;t handle will be hard. The smarter they are, the harder the things they ask you for. And sometimes (often and probably always with AI soon) they are smarter than you at something. So sometimes the questions your team asks are so hard, you don&#8217;t even understand the question, much less have an answer. It might seem like the analogy would break down here, but it doesn&#8217;t, in my experience. As you build up agentic systems, it gets harder and harder to guide them. As soon as you figure out a pattern that comfortably works, you move on to something more ambitious and the models will move right with you, giving you harder problems that they can&#8217;t solve. I don&#8217;t think there&#8217;s a dire warning in this, it&#8217;s just a pattern I am observing. The warning might be that most of you are scratching your heads at how alien this all seems - as you probably should be. The problem, though, is that there are a lot of people out there who are good at this kind of abstract, executive thought. And they are starting to get good leverage and advantage from AI.
