It occurred to me recently that if the old TV show Happy Days (which debuted in 1974) were being made now, it would be about the period around 2005 1 . The original show was being nostalgic for the last few years before the Vietnam war and the sixties - the last &#8220;happy days&#8221; before the world was complicated (really the small pause between the chaotic prior 50 years and the next...). If we made the show now, what would we be nostalgic for? And more importantly, if someone makes one in 20 years, what would they be nostalgic about in our time? What do we see in 2005 that people take for granted, that we know they are about to lose? What do the people in 2045 see us about to lose that we aren&#8217;t aware of? Walking around Tokyo this week, a city I know well, but decided to explore with an AI assistant, maybe helped answer that question. 2005 was pre-iPhone, and most of what we would now think of as social media. I remember the last ordinary moments before we were always connected. You could leave the house and become temporarily unavailable. You could wonder about something without immediately finding out. People had to make plans that survived a few hours without revision. I remember how hard it was to coordinate meeting somewhere - you had to have fallback plans and be very specific. I also remember being at my parents&#8217; cabin in western PA, spending long days on idle tasks like wandering the river, carving a chess set with my cousin, and reading old mysteries and novels I&#8217;d find in my grandparents&#8217; house. Of course, some of that was annoying. I don&#8217;t particularly want to go back to waiting somewhere for someone who has no way to tell me they&#8217;re late. And the first time I visited Tokyo, right before the iPhone, I could barely explore it and had to carry a paper map everywhere - super intimidating if you can&#8217;t speak or read the language. Now, it&#8217;s a much simpler, easier thing, and the city is overrun with tourists who are less brave than I was then (and less culturally aware). I can imagine watching our 2005 show and being nostalgic for that sense of space and isolation. Fast-forward to 2026. In Tokyo, I decided to use an agent to help me explore this week, because left to my own devices, I&#8217;ll do familiar things, and I wanted something new. That part worked well - I found some really interesting off the beaten path things that I loved and hadn&#8217;t known about. But I also felt a lot less present. My typical trips involve a lot of wandering, looking around, and sudden changes of direction. I find it delightful, and I get a better sense of what I&#8217;m immersed in, sometimes. A better feel, anyway. This time, I felt disconnected - just watching a particularly high-res video of Tokyo that happened to make my feet sore. Some of it is the phone and the screen, of course. But I&#8217;ve used phone navigation in the subway for years and it&#8217;s never been a problem. There is a difference between looking up a train route and having someone available to help you think through what to do next. Once I started asking what to do next, there was no obvious point at which to stop asking. It was very easy to slip into some laziness and just let the agent drive. The help was nice to have, but also corrupting. The point of all technology (I hope) is to make our lives better. AI helps us optimize and manage our attention - spending our limited focus on things that help us and that are valuable, and offloading &#8220;toil&#8221; to the machines. This isn&#8217;t particularly a new thing - much of the bookkeeping and management of the world has been on computers for years, and this is why the modern world is richer and faster - we removed a bottleneck. Now we have compute that can help with more complex things that take more of our attention. So I saved some attention on this trip, by having the assistant do planning and research (I even had an interlude in the middle of the trip where I briefly thought about getting a custom-tailored suit and had the agent do research and communication in Japanese). What did I get back with the attention I saved? I got some new and different enjoyment, and new experiences. I also had to manage the agent, which cost a different kind of attention. Overall, I think I was less &#8220;on vacation&#8221; and more working on this trip - it was very hard to ever be disengaged. That feels analogous to the Happy Days example - I don&#8217;t feel like I had much wandering time. But it was better enough that I used it, a lot. The trend is really clear - this will get better and better and get to know me more and more. I will have it more present in my life. Just like it&#8217;s silly and weird to wander around Tokyo without a phone in your pocket (for one thing, you use it to pay everywhere!), it will soon be weird and impossible to do ANYTHING without an agent looking over your shoulder, helping. The connected life becomes the assisted one. Do we want that? I don&#8217;t know - and I&#8217;m not sure it matters. We are highly conflicted about social media, which I personally think is one of the most toxic things we&#8217;ve ever built, and yet we use it deeply and constantly. No one is present in a line, or on a walk - the phones bing, there is always a feed...this will happen, gradually but completely, with AI. It will be more and more helpful, and eventually there will be a time when not being assisted constantly feels as naked as not having your phone...And I already know plenty of people who are utterly immersed in AI, with dozens of accounts running in parallel, thousands of dollars a month in spending&#8230;remember that fomo feeling about the smartphone, or followers&#8230;it&#8217;s coming again. The show we&#8217;d make about 2005 would be about that last disconnected moment in the world, where you had to entertain yourself, find friends in real life, meet people in bars. In 20 years, the show we will make will be about this moment, that last time you had to figure things out by yourself, that you didn&#8217;t have something always with you that knows you, looks out for you, helps you, and suggests to you. We&#8217;ll think it&#8217;s anachronistically funny that people have to do their jobs &#8220;by hand&#8221; with software made for everyone to work the same way. We might think it&#8217;s weird that everyone watches media that someone else made - that might seem as old fashioned as the repressed conservative mores of Happy Days did to my 80&#8217;s self. Who knows? I don&#8217;t know what I think of the trip to Tokyo. I didn&#8217;t love the trip 20 years ago when I couldn&#8217;t explore anything. I&#8217;ve liked my trips since, but they had gotten narrow and repetitive. This was broader and better in a way. But was it mine?
