Fair use benefits everyone
Authors, journalists, creators, academics, accessibility advocates, legal experts, librarians, economists and business leaders are voicing their support for the longstanding principle of fair use and its application to AI training.
For decades, fair use has played a critical role in driving innovation that benefits everyone. It has helped deliver important medical and scientific breakthroughs and new technologies that have made real and positive differences for countless people, including authors and journalists. The New York Times’ lawsuit, meanwhile, asks everyone to ignore a half century of legal history and progress, and toss aside the longstanding principle of fair use in their own narrow pursuit of an undeserved payday.
Experts and leaders across a wide range of fields have recognized that fair use is a win-win that benefits everyone, and have shared their perspectives with the Court in filing briefs in support of fair use.
Below you’ll find excerpts from some of those submissions, in their own words. The full filings are available on the public court docket, and you can read more about fair use and AI here.

AI Progress
The Court should reject Plaintiffs’ attempt to extend copyright beyond its established limits. AI training uses copyrighted works for a fundamentally different purpose, does not provide the public with substitute access to those works, and does not cause the market harm cognizable under the Copyright Act. Instead, it enables a broad array of transformative technologies that are already delivering public benefits. Copyright exists to promote creativity and progress, not to suppress lawful innovation or protect authors from competition. Settled fair use principles therefore compel the conclusion that generative AI training is fair use.
American Council of the Blind, Association of Research Libraries, American Library Association, Consumer Technology Association & Business Software Alliance
This case concerns more than companies that develop large language models (LLMs). It affects medical researchers and patients, educators serving students with different learning needs, people with disabilities seeking greater independence, farmers applying agricultural knowledge, independent creators developing new forms of expression, and industry sectors from software to the law. These communities share an interest in the ability to study and learn from broad and diverse sources of knowledge, and in access to the tools that make that learning useful. Many depend on systems developed by others, including open source AI systems, rather than training models themselves. Fair use supports their ability to learn, discover, communicate, and create. Restrictions on fair use training would affect both the institutions building AI and the people using it.
Authors Alliance
The market dilution theory asks copyright law to protect rightsholders against an economic phenomenon that it has never restricted before. New works can reduce the sales, visibility, or value of older works without copying their protected expression. The Copyright Act deliberately leaves that competition outside the copyright owner’s control. Amicus therefore respectfully asks the Court to hold that “market dilution,” i.e., aggregate competitive pressure from noninfringing outputs, including those that occupy the same subject matter or genre, that share unprotected characteristics, or compete for the same audience, is not cognizable market harm under 17 U.S.C. § 107(4). Rejecting the theory would preserve the distinction the Second Circuit has always maintained: copyright protects authors against substitution for their protected expression, not against the success of new expression that merely competes with it.
Chamber of Progress, Abundance Institute and Trevor Lawrence Jr.
The modern creative industry’s experience with generative AI provides an excellent illustration of these principles in action, demonstrating how generative AI tools advance copyright’s central purpose to enable further human creativity. For example, musicians use generative AI to explore arrangements, develop melodies, and bring projects within reach that limited resources might otherwise foreclose. These tools support and help to effectuate the artist’s judgment, expanding the possibilities for human expression and the works available to the public.
Computer and Communications Industry Association
[T]raining a generative AI model is among the most clearly transformative uses a court has been asked to evaluate. The copyrighted works are not copied to be consumed or distributed; they are analyzed to extract statistical patterns that enable a tool to generate new text or other output at the direction of a human user. Both the purpose and the character of this use are categorically different from the original purpose and character of the underlying works.
Ed Lee and Rebecca Tushnet
Professors of Law
These noninfringing outputs are not substantially similar to Plaintiffs’ works but are simply in the same genre or contain similar facts. Plaintiff’s unprecedented theory of “dilution” turns copyright on its head. Genres and facts are uncopyrightable. Under the Copyright Clause, these elements are not original “writings” of any “author” and are free to copy.
Electronic Frontier Foundation
Plaintiffs are asking the Court to throw that caution to the winds and rewrite copyright law to include a lawful acquisition requirement that finds no basis in statute or case law; and to tilt the fair use analysis against the developer of a general-purpose tool if that tool might be used to create potentially competing non-infringing works. The Court should reject both theories.
Engine and Developers Alliance
Startups’ use of foundation models demonstrates that using copyrighted works to train an AI model is a fair use, not copyright infringement. The purpose of training a foundation model is not to reproduce the works it learns; it is to build a general-purpose tool that others can adapt to new ends. The ends startups have chosen bear no resemblance to the purposes of the works in any training dataset.
Foundation for American Innovation
To the extent AI training enables the creation of new knowledge, expression, software, and other capabilities, those benefits bear on factor four’s assessment of the use’s market effects. That reasoning supports a novel application here: considering the national-security value of capabilities that enable new analysis, software, and other productive work.
Gregory Leppert
