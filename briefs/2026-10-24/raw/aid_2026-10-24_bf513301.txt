Watch the full conversation on YouTube → GPU demand isn’t rectangular A typical capacity contract gives a customer a fixed number of GPUs for a fixed period. Plot capacity against time and you get a rectangle. Real workloads behave differently: they grow, spike, and fall throughout the day. Buy enough for the peak and you pay for idle hardware; buy less and you risk missing demand. Erik describes Modal’s bet as a kind of duration transformation: pool demand across thousands of customers, secure capacity underneath, and let each customer use what they need when they need it. The smoother the aggregate demand, the more useful that pool can be. It’s a useful companion to our conversation with SF Compute’s Evan Conrad : Evan focuses on making capacity tradeable; Erik focuses on making it usable in smaller, elastic slices. There is a financial risk on the other side. If a provider locks in capacity at today’s prices and GPU prices later fall, it can be left holding expensive supply. Erik says he doesn’t expect an imminent collapse, but thinks the possibility matters when building the business. Jump to the GPU price discussion → The sandbox that almost didn’t make it Modal launched sandboxes after Erik and cofounder Akshat Bubna noticed that language models could generate code—and that someone would need a place to run it. Early adoption was modest enough that the team discussed shutting the product down. Then coding agents, background agents, and reinforcement learning created new demand. Erik says sandboxes have grown to roughly a third of Modal’s revenue, a result he didn’t predict at launch. That growth also changes what infrastructure has to do. An agent may create an environment, run code, inspect the result, and try again many times. The latency of starting and restoring those environments becomes part of the experience. In our recent interview with Akshat , we explored this shift from developer experience to agent experience; here Erik adds the product and business story. Modal’s serverless GPU write-up describes the machinery: a buffer of available GPUs, a custom filesystem, process checkpointing, and GPU memory snapshots. Those pieces reduce the work needed to bring another model replica online. It is a concrete example of how infrastructure decisions show up in an application’s responsiveness and cost.
