Winner: &#8216;An engineering manager&#8217;s crazy year&#8217; Edited excerpt from the essay by author of manager.dev , Engineering Manager at HoneyBook: &#8220;It&#8217;s June 2026; a nice, sunny day outside when I sit at my computer, looking at a 10k-line PR opened by my team&#8217;s great designer, and I want to die inside. It&#8217;s not a mock or a prototype; it&#8217;s code he wants to merge into production. Not only is it full of hacky solutions (not even talking about criminal useEffect abuses), it also has dummy data where backend calls were supposed to be. How the hell did I get here? Let&#8217;s back up a bit. I joined HoneyBook as an Engineering Manager in October 2025. It&#8217;s a 13-year-old startup (raised ~$500M), a management platform for business owners (think CRM+contracts+admin, etc.). I joined to lead an existing team of seven experienced engineers responsible for the financial products like managing business expenses, loans, etc. This past year has been a crazy ride, going in just a few months from classic engineering to pure vibe coding, ending with the launch of a real zero-to-one initiative at an established company. Code review collapse We split our work into three repositories: the shared ai-workflows codebase, our own backend repo, and a thin frontend layer called atlas-vibe. Each had a different code review approach: In AI-workflows, every PR was thoroughly reviewed by at least two people including the engineer who wrote the code. In atlas-vibe, there were barely any PRs. We vibe code on the frontend, slowly improving the skills in our repo, and reducing the amount of substandard code a bit. In the backend repo, we had optional code reviews. That&#8217;s an interesting one. Slowly, it became more and more tempting to just not ask for reviews. It&#8217;s addicting to just develop a feature and immediately have it in production. There&#8217;s no need to have an open end in your brain. But still, at that stage I didn&#8217;t accept an &#8216;I don&#8217;t know, Claude wrote it&#8217; approach. Even if you don&#8217;t request a review from someone, it&#8217;s still your responsibility to read and understand it. You can&#8217;t let Claude make the decisions and &#8216;drive&#8217; the work. Output rose like crazy: Source: Weave It took me a while to understand that the code review stage contains a lot of peer pressure. When you submit your code to someone, you feel responsible for understanding it better, and nobody likes getting tons of PR comments (senior engineers, least of all). But when you know that nobody will review it, it&#8217;s much easier to cut corners in your own understanding too. So, in the span of a couple of months, we dropped from two people reviewing and understanding the code, to zero! The result was what you&#8217;d expect: a buggy codebase full of duplications and non-optimal patterns. Hands-on engineering managers (without giving up on the people side) From last October until March this year, I averaged one PR a week. Currently, it&#8217;s 20+. But more importantly, the nature of those PRs has changed: in March-June, it was mainly small things and side features, but since then I&#8217;ve taken on some more serious work myself, and it keeps increasing: Output as normalized units of work for an expert engineer. It&#8217;s been going up. Source: Weave I know there&#8217;s a lot of hate for the demand to go back to being hands-on from EMs, and I really understand it: I believe that people need us more than ever, and EMs who code all day are usually much less effective managers. In May, when we started to work on MyCo, I felt I just had to understand what our new flow looked like, and what we can improve. I believe it&#8217;s almost impossible to do this from the outside. For example, a debate with my engineer about our reduced quality in which I couldn&#8217;t participate properly without seeing the output myself, and I experienced both the advantages and limitations of the new flow. As our careers change, I believe that to be a good EM there is absolutely no other way than to join the building activity. I strongly believe that today, I have found a balance that works for me. I got rid of a lot of tedious work, and I feel as &#8220;present&#8221; as a manager as always, but now 10x more productive in my hands-on work. On Tuesdays and Thursdays I work from home and there are no meetings, and I&#8217;m able to release multiple big features a day.
