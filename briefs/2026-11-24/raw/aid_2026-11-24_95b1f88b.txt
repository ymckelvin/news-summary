Today&#8217;s post is a live blog of Class 21 of my graduate seminar &#8220;Forecasting: A Critical Retrospective.&#8221; In this class, we reflected on the semester and discussed things we should have covered. Today&#8217;s post is about one of those topics: qualitative forecasts. The syllabus and list of past posts are here . In Sources of Power , Gary Klein lays out the impressive power and remarkable complexity of human decision making under uncertainty. He trails experts of all stripes, from firefighters to doctors to chess players, and finds they are not rational optimizers but finely tuned pattern recognizers. When I first read this book, I thought that I should be able to build machine learning systems to do this. What are machine learning systems other than pattern recognizers? After six months of futile effort, I realized I was being silly. Human pattern recognition isn&#8217;t empirical risk minimization. Indeed, what makes us so interesting and powerful is our deviation from normative mathematical models of rational choice. Key to Klein&#8217;s model is the ability to rapidly build qualitative forecasts from experience. People use complex imagination and storytelling to rapidly diagnose how situations fit into their past experiences, and match their subsequent actions. Their forecasts are not numerical, but instead plausible stories developed from years of experience, tutelage, and tacit trial and error. In their book Radical Uncertainty , John Kay and Mervyn King call such experientially grounded qualitative forecasts reference narratives . Reference narratives are stories that express people&#8217;s realistic expectations of uncertain scenarios. The reference narrative is more general than Klein&#8217;s rapid-fire story generation. For example, Kay and King explain that &#8220;[f]or J. P. Morgan, the overarching reference narrative is one in which the bank continues profitable growth.&#8221; The reference narratives are whatever stories a decision maker assesses as plausible for the purpose of making new decisions. In these narratives, &#8220;[t]he meaning of risk is specific to the individual, household or institution concerned.&#8221; Shared stories, imaginings, and narratives are core to our design and implementation of engineered systems, policy frameworks, and institutional plans. These stories do forecast potential future outcomes, but they seldom need numbers. As we&#8217;ve seen this semester, numbers can bring intersubjective agreement with an air of objectivity, but they are not objective by fiat. Numerical and probabilistic forecasts can be useful. They are just not exclusively useful. They are one drawer in a giant tool chest for complex decision making. One consistent theme of this class was that forecasts help us imagine what we&#8217;ll do when something goes wrong. We aim to conjure up unknown unknowns into knowns by creating appropriate reference narratives. Organizations put structures in place so teams can anticipate what might happen. Blameless postmortems exist to figure out how to make sure unexpected bad events don&#8217;t happen again. Numerical forecasts and KPIs are often less valuable than mapping out design spaces and going through potential failures. So much of design is about sketching out what is possible. What is probable is always secondary. You can&#8217;t assign a probability to a story you couldn&#8217;t envision. In my class with Deb Raji on machine learning evaluation , we wanted to figure out what it means for an engineered system to work. I left that class with a simple characterization : &#8220;A system works if it doesn&#8217;t yet not work.&#8221; I wrote, &#8220;We create a system of checks and metrics, find a system that matches these, and run with it until we encounter an exception. When we encounter such an exception, we figure out which part of our design made a wrong assumption and patch the design. By keeping a tally of what has worked in the past, this design process becomes faster in the future.&#8221; In the language of today&#8217;s post, we construct a reference narrative around which our current system or policy will be effective, and we operate until we find a violation of that narrative that we didn&#8217;t anticipate. We then patch our story with this new information, build new future stories, and trudge ahead. This iterative patching is what Max Raginsky and I think a &#8220;Lakatosian Defense&#8221; of engineering might look like. Like progressive scientific programs, an engineering system that doesn&#8217;t violate exceptions and whose reference narrative leads to continued beneficial operation is &#8220;working.&#8221; And like Lakatos&#8217; view of the progressive research program, when we encounter an exception to the reference narrative, we don&#8217;t need to throw the system away. The unknown unknown is always real. When it manifests itself, you change your narrative. It&#8217;s only when we can&#8217;t figure out a patch, either because the outcome was too catastrophic or because we&#8217;re unable to determine a new story, that an engineering system degenerates and we abandon a design program altogether. 1 Reference narratives and their forecasts don&#8217;t need to be backed by numbers. Numbers and probabilities are potentially useful, but if we only work with what can be quantified, we block off huge sets of possible futures for us to imagine. We&#8217;ve all seen a downside of overreliance on statistical forecasts of the future. 2 People refuse to let reality get in the way of a good mathematical model. Too often, when bad futures arise, math modelers lean on &#8220;we said there was a chance&#8221; and are tied to the idea that a decision can be right even if the outcome was bad because the math says so. With a quantified probabilistic model, we can always argue post hoc that any future fits within our predicted uncertainty, and hence that we weren&#8217;t wrong. This stubborn desire to defend our mathematical models prevents us from patching our reference narrative. It walls us off from learning from experience and creating new narratives, and dooms us to miss the next tragedy too.
