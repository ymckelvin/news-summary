We are bringing AI agents to the wet-lab bench at Google Research to navigate complex biological landscapes. By grounding agents in physical lab robotics, we can run fast closed-loop molecular design cycles where agents learn how to learn, ultimately discovering single-digit nanomolar protein binders for cancer targets.
Frontier AI systems have the potential to transform biology research and drug discovery by intelligently navigating vast combinatorial search spaces (e.g., a single 100-amino-acid protein spans at least 20100 possible sequences) calling for dynamic multi-candidate exploration. Our long-term research vision is that AI agents will not only ideate and analyze, but also run lab experiments to test their hypotheses, learn from the results, and self-improve to solve complex problems — all driven by human scientific agency.
Realizing this vision requires solving two intertwined challenges: First, frontier models still do not reason reliably about 3-dimensional geometry and physical state — which poses a problem in the automation of the specialized liquid-handling robots that move reagents, track volumes, and execute molecular biology protocols in a wet lab. Second, while agentic hypothesis generation and in-silico design are advancing rapidly, needing repeated cycles of real-world feedback to validate the proposals and navigate vast search spaces, real-world wet-lab experiments have not kept pace, making experimental readouts more of a bottleneck than ever.
In this post we share three research projects highlighting our progress on bridging the gap between in-silico AI exploration and real-world wet-lab experimentation:
First, we present the VERified Infrastructure for Discovery Laboratories (VERID) framework to bridge the gap between the high-level reasoning of AI agents and the complex, error-prone physical reality of a wet lab. It is a semantic digital twin of the laboratory workcell, i.e., a live, stateful 3D volumetric entity tree that tracks everything from robotic arm geometry to individual well volumes, reagent identities, and pipette tip inventory.
The safety of a command depends on the live physical context as well as the code. An agent can generate syntactically valid code that is physically impossible (e.g., lowering an 8-channel pipette into a narrow trough that is too short), logistically invalid (e.g., aspirating from a depleted well), or scientifically compromised by unmodeled fluidic effects.

In our report, we show these capabilities in action through case studies. First, synchronized video and execution traces caught uneven dye stratification caused by dispensing too close to the well floor, allowing the agent to refine its mixing strategy. In the same campaign, the persistent digital twin state allowed the agent to recover mid-run from a plate-reader failure without re-pipetting. Second, when the simulator rejected an 8-channel aspiration that physically wouldn't fit in a narrow trough, the agent replanned on the fly and designed a targeted factorial diagnostic isolating a subtle bias caused by residual tip-wall carryover that replication alone would have missed. Finally, in a closed-loop self-play campaign, an agent iteratively designed and ran robot experiments to reconstruct blinded dye mixtures from overhead camera images, identifying its own optical and fluidic artifacts across runs to improve accuracy more than fivefold.
Reproducible execution and full trace logs — capturing procedural variables like dwell times, evaporation, and plate layout — lay the groundwork for agents to eventually be able to separate biological signals from experimental noise as they explore and refine many candidates in parallel across rounds.
Agents need real experimental readouts to refine their hypotheses as computational models do not capture the full picture of what happens in a wet-lab reaction and cannot replace empirical validation. In “Aptamers as a sandbox for lab-in-the-loop agentic molecular discovery”, we use protein-binding DNA aptamers to prototype the lab in a closed loop with AI agents over 3 design-test-iteration cycles. Aptamer discovery is a type of experiment common in research efforts for clinical biomarker discovery. A complete aptamer design-test-analyze cycle takes just 7–10 days, as DNA can be chemically synthesized in pooled libraries and read out directly by sequencing. In each round, agents design candidate sequences, which are screened against target proteins on magnetic beads to isolate functional binders, and then sequenced to return rich quantitative data for the next cycle.
Across three rounds on two protein targets with varying difficulty levels (myoglobin and α-thrombin), AI-driven rational and de novo design raised confirmed on-target hit rates as can be seen in the image below (to 33.5% and 42.1% for myoglobin and α-thrombin, respectively), though each target required identifying and overcoming a different wet-lab failure mode.
On α-thrombin, Round 1 yielded 0% binders because fixed primer overhangs disrupted folding in 48% of sequences; once the agents introduced clamped flanking stems in Round 2 and diversified in Round 3, hit rates climbed to 26.0% and then 42.1% at 98.5% specificity.
On myoglobin, as agents prioritized raw binding affinity in Round 2, they heavily enriched G-quadruplex motifs (63% of designs). While raw pull-down increased, the wet-lab data showed that most of these G-quadruplexes were non-specific "sticky" binders adhering to off-target proteins and bare magnetic beads, causing both target specificity (from 75.0% to 19.1%) and true, bead-corrected hit rate (from 10.2% to 3.7%) to plunge. Recognizing this artifact from the Round 2 readout, the agents autonomously revised their design rules for Round 3, capping promiscuous motifs, subtracting bead binders, and pivoting to non-G4 structural scaffolds to recover specificity (72.4%) and surge ninefold in on-target hit rate (33.5%).
In the paper, we also examine how agents reason like scientists, analyzing data and suggesting tweaks to wet-lab protocols, but also sometimes think atypically, exploring sequence spaces that structural biologists might not.
Closing the loop for proteins is more complex. Unlike aptamers, which are chemically synthesized, proteins must be expressed biologically, so each loop adds cellular or cell-free selection, expression, and folding steps. Together with a larger sequence space, this creates bottlenecks on both the computational and wet-lab sides.
In “Agent-guided epitope excision enables economical de novo binder design”, we applied our workflow to fibroblast activation protein alpha (FAP), a large, challenging receptor overexpressed in solid tumors. In just a few experimental runs, the top designs yielded binders with single-digit nanomolar affinity, a level of tight, drug-like binding typically achieved only after extensive rounds of laboratory optimization.
Computationally, de novo binder design requires generating candidate backbones, designing sequences to fit them, and re-folding the resulting complexes to predict binding. That final re-folding step consumes the vast majority of GPU compute, as it scales quadratically with target length. By surgically excising just the local binding pocket during folding, our pipeline accelerated each cycle more than eightfold without losing structural accuracy. This turns decisions about where to bind and how much surrounding structure to include into tactical levers that LLM agents are well-suited to optimize.
