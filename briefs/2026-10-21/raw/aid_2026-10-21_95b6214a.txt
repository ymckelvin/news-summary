A software engineer whose company is looking into its own spending on AI tools pinged me with a very timely question recently, asking what other companies spend on AI, per engineer. They were also keen to find out what the PR throughput was before and after the spending on AI at other places. Spending on AI agents is ballooning at all the tech companies I talk with this year. With 2027 coming around fast, many companies are seeking to put a realistic number on how much they really spend on AI. The change in PR throughput is a very simple metric, and one of those &#8220;readily-available&#8221; measurements at nearly all companies, It&#8217;s also one that probably has not been &#8220;gamed&#8221; by devs yet, and it&#8217;s possible to compare a &#8220;before AI&#8221; and &#8220;after AI&#8221; measurement with this metric. To find out more and to try and answer the reader&#8217;s question, I asked around and got to talk with engineering leaders and senior+ engineers at 27 workplaces. I also gathered aggregate data from CodeRabbit and Gitar. This article summarizes what I found, covering: Stats from CodeRabbit and Gitar. Weekly PRs by devs using the AI code review tool increased by around 2x compared to a year ago. Meanwhile, Gitar sees &#8220;AI-native&#8221; teams ship 60+ PRs/dev/week. AI Spending patterns of 27 companies I got details on AI spending and changes in the rate of PRs from 27 different companies. I anonymized most names by request. At a glance, here&#8217;s how spend looks like: Spend split across the 27 companies A few common themes from this data set: Company size and spend on AI are unrelated. In the data set, a 2-person .NET startup spends $3,000/month/dev, and a publicly traded company with 1,500 engineers, limits devs to $150 of AI spend per month. Spend peaked during the summer, and is falling. June and July saw peak monthly spend at nearly all companies, and costs have roughly halved for those on API plans. A mix of Opus 5.5 and moving to open models is the reason for the drop at most companies. Spending caps are common at medium-and-above spenders. It&#8217;s normal to have a spending cap in place at medium-and-above spenders, where additional spend needs to be justified. Most people say the caps are easily avoided. Subscriptions conceal concrete AI costs, but some subsidies are ending. Several companies mentioned that their subsidized AI subscriptions are coming to an end, so they need to prepare to either optimize costs, or have their bill increase 5-10x. Claude and Opus 5.5 seem to be the favorite models. Within this rather limited data, Claude and Opus 5.5 dominated answers about where most of the spending goes. Cursor, Codex and GitHub Copilot were also mentioned. 3. High spenders ($1,000+ per month) Here are details of companies spending considerable amounts each month on AI coding tools. Quant trading shop, ~5,000 staff (circa 2,000 devs) This company has seen its AI spend balloon overall: total spend was $8M /month in September, a 16x increase on the $500K/month spend in January! The average spend is $1,600 /dev/month (up from around $80 in January). Most are going to Claude, and the biggest spenders are not devs, but traders. A current engineer there told me: &#8220;Lots of traders are using Claude. Engineering is learning the importance of rate limiting for traders who accidentally run expensive queries without realizing it.&#8221; The company has a $25,000 limit per employee for AI, which is extremely high, more than at NVIDIA! The engineer explained this generous limit: &#8220;The last few years have, generally, been extremely profitable for trading firms. For us, last year was our most profitable year ever &#8211; and we&#8217;re already on track to beat it this year. So, the spend on AI is well within what these companies can afford.&#8221; AI outlay is expected to grow: this firm is building its own internal GPU and CPU farms to run models, and are building models expected to beat state-of-the-art (SOTA) models like Fable, according to their own data. ROI is measured differently at trading firms. The ROI of any spend is the profit or loss made by a trading desk, and AI spend is now part of the costbase. So, provided the desk&#8217;s profit increases, no questions are expected. Small .NET startup, 2 devs, EU Outside of Big Tech where the monthly AI spend can be $10K+ for some people, the largest per-developer bill comes from a two-person .NET shop in Europe. This agency builds assessment games for a niche, global HR platform. Everything is built in C# and Blazor, running on Azure. A single tech stack was a great decision, especially now, they told me. $3,000 /month/dev (&#8364;2,500/dev/month) is what they spend on AI tools. Most of the spend goes on Claude Opus, and GitHub Copilot. One of the devs told me: &#8220;We barely type code anymore. We do a lot of feature development, but also a ton of backlog cleanup. Most of our systems are 5-10 years old. We do about 60 PRs/dev/week (so around 10-15/day). It&#8217;s amazing how fast we&#8217;re clearing out our ~10 years-old backlog. If you have high agency and know what to build, these are fun times!&#8221; PR frequency: up 5-10x. As this engineer told me: &#8220;We used to do 1-2 PRs per day, and now it&#8217;s 10+. It feels like our two-person team is doing the work that would have taken about 5-7 people before AI.&#8221; Series D, hospitality sector, 1,000 employees, ~200 engineers, US $1,500 /dev/month is the default spending cap, but devs can ask for an increase.
