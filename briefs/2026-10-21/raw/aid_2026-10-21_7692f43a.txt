Last year , Alex Lieberman and his cofounder joined us to explain Tenex&#8217;s unusual bet: pay AI engineers for the work they deliver, rather than the hours they spend at a keyboard. If AI makes an engineer faster, an hourly rate can make the engineer less money for the same job. Tenex wanted the incentives to run the other way. At AIE New York, Alex came back with CJ Hess to talk about what happens when you scale that idea into an engineering organization. We also put one of our own experiments on the table: Kill My SaaS , where we asked builders to replace a conference speaker-management tool we were quoted $43,000 a year to use. Both stories raise the same question: when building gets cheaper, how do you decide what good work is worth? Paying for output when the job keeps changing Tenex still uses story points as an important part of engineering compensation. Alex says the feared failure modes&#8212;people inflating estimates or shipping bad code to rack up points&#8212;haven&#8217;t been the biggest problem. Tenex&#8217;s AI strategists are responsible for customer retention and growth, giving them an incentive to push back on work that doesn&#8217;t serve the client. The harder question is how to reward work that doesn&#8217;t fit neatly into a ticket: understanding a customer&#8217;s problem, designing an architecture, or deciding what should be built in the first place. CJ describes spending less time directly coding as models get better, and more time getting the customer&#8217;s context into a form the model can use. A compensation system designed around shipped tasks has to make room for that work too. Alex says Tenex has grown to 75 people, with customers split between Fortune 2000 companies and mid-market businesses. The firm started as an AI-powered development shop; increasingly, customers are asking for help with AI transformation across the company. The team that won with our human judges came 19th on our AI judge&#8217;s leaderboard. It was a useful reminder that checking whether software satisfies a list of scenarios and judging whether people want to use it are different jobs. I brought the project to Alex as a real example of a company choosing to build its own software. His pushback: the further upmarket you go, the more expensive and risky it becomes to rip out an established system. And even for a smaller tool, someone has to maintain it. Is the saving big enough to justify that ongoing attention? My answer is that the upside isn&#8217;t just the bill. Our team has lived with a mediocre product for a long time. If we own the workflow, we can make it better for speakers and operators and iterate in minutes instead of waiting months for a vendor. That potential has to be weighed against the maintenance Alex describes. Watch the exchange &#8594; The case for&#8212;and against&#8212;vertical AI Alex is generally bearish on vertical-specific AI products: if every enterprise expects a one-of-one solution, how do you deliver that at software margins? And as frontier models improve, how much of the underlying task gets absorbed by general-purpose tools? I pushed back with examples like Harvey and OpenEvidence. Domain-specific data, specialized interfaces, integrations and user habits can matter even when the underlying model is available to everyone. Bloomberg is a useful comparison: a trader can get market data elsewhere, but the terminal brings data, workflows, communication and existing habits together. CJ draws a distinction between a vertical product whose main feature is an agent performing a task, and a tool that improves the actual workflow around that task. That leaves a more interesting question than whether &#8220;vertical AI&#8221; wins or loses: which products become a durable place people do their work, and which become a feature of the agent they already use?
