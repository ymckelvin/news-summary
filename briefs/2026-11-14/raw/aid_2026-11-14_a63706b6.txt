Now, onto today&#8217;s article. Sean Allen is a software engineer with deep expertise in building large distributed systems, stream processing, and confidential computing. He is a core contributor to the programming language Pony , which is designed for concurrency. Sean is a distinguished engineer at Antithesis. I was keen to talk with Sean due to his work on confidential computing at Microsoft, as well as his extensive work on the Pony language, and his passion for software correctness, which he works on fulltime at Antithesis. For this conversation, we set ourselves the challenge of discussing modern software engineering without using the phrase &#8220;AI&#8221; a single time. Today we cover: Software correctness &amp; languages built for correctness. Why languages in the Meta Language (ML) family that are built around strong type systems catch more errors during compilation time than those in the Algorithmic Language (ALGOL) family. Does anybody care about testing? For someone passionate about correctness, Sean is surprisingly anti-testing. He argues that manual testing is the worst way to ensure correctness because you want illegal states to be prohibited from being expressed in a language &#8211; even before testing. Workloads &amp; correctness. A more robust way of testing stuff than things like unit tests, is to define &#8220;workloads&#8221; that can be executed numerous times during a test. A common workload example is The Bank Test to stress test database isolation guarantees. Is quality a &#8220;gate&#8221; or not? It&#8217;s tempting to think of quality checks as closed gates that keep out poor quality. Sean pushes back against this; he says it&#8217;s important to consider quality from the very start, and to build with validation techniques that provide feedback on correctness. He prefers this to adding CI/CD quality &#8220;gates&#8221; late in the development process. Tech debt only hurts if you have to pay it back. Stop being afraid of tech debt because you often don&#8217;t need to pay it back at all! Sean says it&#8217;s common that tech debt is disregarded during rewrites when a system has become popular. No good fix for supply chain security. Supply chain attacks &#8211; when a package gets hijacked by an attacker and malicious code is executed on a machine or service &#8211; are very hard to defend against because languages with truly robust defenses, like &#8216;E&#8217;, are unpopular, while popular languages assume a trustworthy ecosystem. There&#8217;s no single or simple fix, says Sean. Garbage collection is slow &#8211; but doesn&#8217;t have to be. A summary of why garbage collection is devastating for runtime performance, and how Sean built a predictably performant approach in the Pony language. Go slow to go fast. Counter-intuitive advice: spend more time designing upfront and aligning everyone, and then you&#8217;ll move fast! Software correctness &amp; languages built for correctness Gergely: How do you define &#8216;software correctness&#8217;? Sean: &#8220;It&#8217;s down to whether my program does what I expect. I tend to talk about things in terms of &#8216;invariants,&#8217; which is a more formal specification term. An invariant is a guarantee that my software does what&#8217;s expected of it. But here&#8217;s the problem: in lots of cases, people don&#8217;t know what they expect their software to do upfront! They know when it&#8217;s not working, but they don&#8217;t necessarily know what it means for it to be working. &#8216;Correct&#8217; software does its business purpose and doesn&#8217;t wake anybody up at 2 a.m. with an outage. That is correct enough for me.&#8221; Sean Allen (right) discusses software correctness Your personal definition of &#8216;beautiful code&#8217; mirrors this. Sean: &#8220;I was on the CoRecursive podcast when the subject of beautiful code in Ruby came up. I didn&#8217;t really know what people meant when they said &#8216;beautiful code&#8217;. Eventually, I came up with my own definition: beautiful code serves the purpose for which it was written and does not wake anyone up at 2 a.m.. That&#8217;s beautiful code to me!&#8221; Early in your career, you programmed in the Ada language. What did that teach? Sean: &#8220;When I was learning to program, I decided to pick a language and dedicate myself to it for a while. I ended up settling on Ada and C. NASA and the US Defense Department were the types who this language was aimed at. Ada still exists today, and the pitch that Ada companies make is &#8216;so, you like Rust, how would you like more correctness guarantees?&#8217; One of my great regrets is I could not get programs to compile in Ada because I didn&#8217;t know much about programming back then. It was so frustrating! Meanwhile, I could get C programs to compile, but they were segfaulting all the time! Over the years, I developed an intuition for how to do things in C so that my programs would not segfault and die. I had all these rules for writing concurrent C and C++ correctly. And even though I became really rigorous, I&#8217;m human and screwed up stuff. C gave me almost nothing in terms of correctness. It was just a big gun that I pointed at my own foot and shot it off with, over and over again.
